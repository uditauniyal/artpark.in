A result in the lab tells you what might be possible. Or an end user might tell you what is required. Making it real takes another body of work — and much of that work is invention too, working through an iterative cycle of building, breaking and iterating.

The series

Three ways a breakthrough travels.

Research translation is not one pipeline. Sometimes it means completing a system around an invention. Sometimes it means building an enterprise around a scientific capability. Sometimes it means creating a platform that other people build on. Each story follows one of those paths — what happened, who did the work, and what it took.

These are historical case studies. ARTPARK was not involved in any of them. We study them because they show, in detail, what the work beyond a breakthrough looks like.

Complete the system

The light bulb was only the beginning.

On the afternoon of 4 September 1882, about 400 electric lamps came on in lower Manhattan. A practical lamp had existed for almost three years. What took so long was everything around it: dynamos, miles of underground copper, fuses, meters, switches, factories, a franchise from the city — and a price that could compete with gas.

The story usually told is about a filament. The more useful one is about a system — and how many people, in how many countries, it took to build.

What we believe

The work between research and real-world use is not the paperwork after innovation. It is part of innovation itself.

Technologies have to be built, tested, challenged, integrated and refined in the environments where they will finally be used. What we learned from the three stories, in four sentences:

A working result is where the questions start.

A practical lamp glowed at Menlo Park in late 1879. Whether a city could afford to run on it was a separate question — one that took until 1882 to answer, and then only in part.

From the light bulb story →

Test it where it will live.

Real environments add requirements no lab result can satisfy on its own. When Strand moved from research software into diagnostics, it needed a wet lab, sample handling, clinical relationships and external accreditation — a different company built around the same science.

From the Strand story →

Not every result should become a startup.

Some research becomes a product, some becomes a firm, and some becomes infrastructure that thousands of others build on. GPS created its widest value as a free public signal — not as anyone's single product.

From the GPS story →

The unglamorous work is invention too.

Edison's three-wire distribution scheme sharply reduced the copper needed to deliver power — a cost problem solved by engineering, not by the lamp. Integration, reliability and economics deserve the same respect as the original insight.
